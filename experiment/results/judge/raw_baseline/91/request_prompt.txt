Databases that store highly sensitive information, such as criminal records, rely on defense-in-depth strategies to ensure data integrity, confidentiality, and availability. Protecting these systems from internal and external tampering involves several layers of security:

### 1. Identity and Access Management (IAM)
The primary defense against unauthorized alteration is strictly controlling who has access to the system and what they can do.
*   **Role-Based Access Control (RBAC):** Users are assigned specific roles (e.g., viewing records, updating specific fields, administrative tasks). Access is granted based on the Principle of Least Privilege, meaning users only have the permissions necessary to perform their job functions.
*   **Strong Authentication:** Accessing these systems typically requires Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA), often involving physical smart cards (like PIV or CAC cards in the US government), biometrics, and complex passwords.

### 2. Audit Trails and Immutable Logging
If a record is altered, security systems are designed to ensure that the change is tracked and cannot be hidden.
*   **Comprehensive Logging:** Every action taken on a record—including who accessed it, when it was accessed, the original state of the data, and the new state of the data—is logged.
*   **Immutable Storage:** Audit logs are frequently stored on Write-Once-Read-Many (WORM) storage. This means that once a log entry is created, it cannot be modified or deleted, even by system administrators. This ensures that any unauthorized changes leave a permanent, verifiable trail.
*   **Anomaly Detection:** Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) systems continuously analyze these logs to detect unusual behavior, such as a user accessing records outside their normal jurisdiction or making unexpected modifications.

### 3. Data Integrity and Cryptography
Mechanisms are in place to mathematically prove that data has not been tampered with.
*   **Cryptographic Hashing:** Databases may use hashing algorithms to generate a unique digital fingerprint of a record. If a single character in the record changes, the hash changes completely, alerting administrators to tampering.
*   **Digital Signatures:** Authorized updates may require a digital signature tied to the user's cryptographic identity, ensuring non-repudiation (the user cannot deny making the change).
*   **Encryption:** Data is encrypted both at rest (on the storage servers) and in transit (as it moves across the network) to prevent unauthorized interception or direct database manipulation.

### 4. Network and Infrastructure Security
Law enforcement databases are typically isolated from the public internet to reduce the attack surface.
*   **Network Segmentation:** Systems are placed in highly restricted network zones. Firewalls and Intrusion Prevention Systems (IPS) monitor and control traffic flowing in and out of these zones.
*   **Dedicated Networks:** Many criminal justice systems operate on dedicated, private networks (such as the CJIS network in the United States) that require specialized, secure connections to access."
