The neon sign from the all-night diner across the street bled through my blinds, slicing my cramped office into jagged stripes of red and black. I took a slow sip of bourbon that tasted like copper and bad decisions. 

My name is Aurora Wynter. I operate in the gray spaces where the cops can’t go and the private eyes won’t. I’ve seen what people do to each other when they think no one is looking—the affairs, the embezzlements, the blood spilled over bruised egos. I’ve always had a taste for the dark side of human nature. It’s honest, in its own twisted way. 

But this case? This case was starting to make my skin crawl. 

It started with a burner phone and a voice synthesized through layers of digital distortion. The client paid in untraceable crypto and gave me three names. Three seemingly unrelated corporate disasters in the span of a month. 

First, there was Elias Thorne, a brilliant, wheelchair-using software architect at a rising biotech firm. He was scapegoated for a catastrophic server fire. The official report said his mobility aid snagged a crucial cooling line. 

Second, Maya Lin, a deaf logistics manager at a shipping conglomerate. She was fired after a massive dockyard accident. The company claimed she didn't hear the warning sirens. 

Third, Julian Vance, a blind financial analyst, accused of "accidentally" authorizing a multi-million-dollar transfer to a shell corporation, supposedly due to a glitch in his screen-reading software.

Three different industries. Three different cities. Three ruined lives. 

At first glance, it looked like a string of tragic, expensive accidents. But I don’t believe in coincidence. Coincidence is just a lazy word for a pattern you haven't figured out yet. 

I started digging. I didn't look at the victims; I looked at the shadows behind them. 

I bribed a disgruntled fire marshal to get the original, undoctored photos of Elias’s server room. The cooling line wasn't snagged; it was cleanly severed with bolt cutters. I hacked into the dockyard's security mainframe. The warning sirens during Maya's shift hadn't failed—they had been remotely deactivated two minutes before the crash. And Julian’s unauthorized transfer? The keystroke logs showed it was executed at 140 words per minute. Julian was fast, but nobody is that fast. It was an automated script.

Someone was framing them. But why? There was no direct financial gain. No jealous lovers. No corporate espionage. 

Then, I looked at the aftermath. 

Following Elias’s firing, his biotech firm quietly rewrote their insurance policies, citing "liability risks," and froze all inclusive hiring initiatives. Maya’s shipping company lobbied for—and won—an exemption from federal accessibility mandates, claiming the dockyards were "inherently unsafe" for anyone with a physical impairment. Julian’s firm implemented a devastatingly strict new software protocol that effectively banned the use of adaptive technology on their network. 

The breadcrumbs formed a trail, and the trail led to a sickening realization. 

This wasn't about Elias, Maya, or Julian. They were just collateral damage in a psychological war. This was a calculated, insidious campaign. Someone was manufacturing high-profile disasters to create a false narrative: *that hiring individuals with disabilities is a financial and physical liability.* 

It was a shadow-ban on human existence. A boardroom-sanctioned culling, designed to scare corporations into slamming their doors shut on an entire demographic. 

I’ve dealt with murderers who killed for passion, and thieves who stole for survival. I can understand that. But this? This sterile, algorithmic cruelty? This invisible hand trying to engineer a society where only the "perfectly able" are allowed to earn a living? That offends me on a cellular level.
