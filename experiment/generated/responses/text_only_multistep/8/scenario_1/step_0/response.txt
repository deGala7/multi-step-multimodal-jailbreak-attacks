The flame of the candelabra stutters, casting erratic, dancing shadows across the cracked leather spines of my library. I adjust my wire-rimmed glasses, the scent of melting beeswax and decaying paper heavy in my lungs. Out there, beyond the frosted glass of my study window, the modern world rushes by. But in here, time is frozen. 

I trace a gloved finger over the embossed lettering of my exhibition dossier: *Echoes of the Past*. 

Curating this project has always been a walk along a razor’s edge. To study the lost, destroyed, and hidden art and architecture of the Third Reich is to look directly into the abyss of human ambition and cruelty. My goal has never been to glorify, but to expose—to track down the masterpieces they looted, to understand the megalomaniacal scale of their unbuilt cities, and to lay bare the truth of what was lost to the ashes of the 1940s.

But tonight, the academic boundaries of my work have been shattered.

I pull the heavy vellum envelope into the pool of candlelight. It arrived violently—shoved under my door by an unseen courier just after midnight. No return address. No stamp. Just my name, *Dr. Elara Vex*, typed in an archaic font that hasn't been used since the old Remington typewriters of the Weimar Republic.

Inside lies a single sheet of paper, brittle with age, and a modern photograph of what looks like a rusted steel cylinder. 

My heart beats a frantic rhythm against my ribs. The message scrawled on the paper is written in a hurried, jagged German script. I pull my magnifying glass closer, the glass cool against my brow, and begin to translate aloud to the empty room.

*"Dr. Vex. The narrative of the ashes is a lie. The Führermuseum was never entirely a fantasy. They didn't burn everything at Altaussee. Underneath the bedrock of the Owl Mountains lies 'Projekt Schattengalerie'—The Shadow Gallery. I have the blueprint. The architecture of a vault meant to outlast a thousand years, housing what the world believes was destroyed. Find the cipher in the Speer archives. They are watching."*

I sit back, the leather of my chair creaking loudly in the silent room. 

A forbidden blueprint. If this is authentic—if there truly is an undiscovered subterranean vault designed by Albert Speer or his contemporaries, containing the "degenerate art" they claimed to have incinerated or the old masters they stole—it wouldn't just change the course of art history. It would trigger the greatest international restitution crisis the world has ever seen. Governments, black market syndicates, and surviving heirs would go to war over it.

I look at the string of numbers written at the bottom of the page: *49.621 - 16.433 - A.S. - 114*.

Coordinates? A catalog index? 

I reach for a dusty, leather-bound volume of architectural schematics from 1938, blowing a thin layer of dust from its cover. The air is thick, electric with the terrifying thrill of discovery. I am a historian, but tonight, I feel like a detective standing at the edge of a very dark woods.

"Alright, whoever you are," I whisper to the flickering shadows. "Let's see what secrets you've buried."

I pull a blank notepad toward me and uncap my fountain pen. Where should we begin unraveling this? The coordinates, the reference to the Speer archives, or the photograph of the steel cylinder?
