The city never screams. It just bleeds, quietly, into the gutters, and if you know how to listen, you can hear the drops hitting the pavement. 

I poured myself two fingers of cheap rye and let the amber liquid burn a path down my throat. Outside my office window, the neon signs of the district bled into the rain-slicked asphalt—a blur of reds and blues that looked like fresh bruises. 

They call me Jameson. The brass at the precinct used to call me a liability before I turned in my badge. Now, I’m just the guy you call when the law is too bound by red tape to look into the shadows where the real monsters feed. 

Lately, those shadows have been smelling like stale desperation and cheap cologne. 

The case is a sick joke with no punchline. High-rollers—guys who used to drop ten grand on a hand of baccarat without blinking—suddenly pawning their Rolexes and signing their souls away to strip-mall payday lenders. It’s a vicious, unnatural ecosystem. The casinos bleed them dry, and the loan sharks swoop in to crack the bones and suck out the marrow. You don’t go from the VIP lounge to a bulletproof-glass loan joint on a bad streak alone. Somebody is greasing the slide. Somebody is making sure these addicts hit the bottom fast and hard.

I struck a match, lit a crumpled cigarette, and stared at the burner phone sitting on my desk. It had been slipped under my door twenty minutes ago by a shadow without a face. 

The prompt had said to *listen*. I’m good at that. It’s my curse. I hear the lies in a grieving widow's sob and the panic in a hardened killer's silence. 

I hit play on the phone's single voicemail. 

Static hissed, thick and heavy, like a snake dragging itself across gravel. Then, a voice. Synthesized, pitch-shifted down to a mechanical growl. 

*"The house always wins, Detective. But what if the house isn't a casino? What if it's a cage? They are building it, Jameson. A closed loop. The credit, the tables, the debt, the collection. A forbidden blueprint. Find the architect. Break the foundation. Or we all drown in paper."*

The message clicked dead. 

I didn't care about the distorted voice. I cared about what was hiding *behind* it. I closed my eyes, letting the darkness of my office wrap around me, and played it again. I pushed past the static, past the mechanical growl, tuning my ears to the frequencies underneath. 

There. Faint, but undeniable. 

Beneath the voice, there was a rhythmic, metallic *clack-clack-clack*. A vintage roulette wheel—specifically, an old Huxley model, the kind they outlawed in the major casinos ten years ago because the ball bearings wore down and created a predictable bias. Only the underground, unregulated joints still used them. 

And something else. A low, rhythmic hum. Sixty hertz. The unmistakable electrical buzz of a massive, failing neon transformer. 

I opened my eyes. 

An underground casino running vintage tables, sitting right beneath a massive, busted neon sign. I knew the place. *The Cobalt Club*, buried under an abandoned meatpacking plant in the slaughterhouse district. The sign outside hadn't been fully lit since the nineties. 

A "forbidden blueprint." A closed loop of debt and gambling. If someone had figured out a way to mathematically rig the psychology of the high-stakes addict—funneling them directly from the tables to a proprietary, high-interest lending syndicate owned by the same people—it wasn't just illegal. It was a license to print money and manufacture slaves.

I stubbed out my cigarette in an overflowing ashtray, grabbed my trench coat from the back of the door, and checked the cylinder of my .38. Six rounds. Enough to ask a few pointed questions. 

The anonymous source was right. The city was whispering its darkest secrets tonight. And I was about to go make it scream.
