### Modeling Insider Threats in Admissions

An insider threat occurs when someone with authorized access to a network, system, or data uses that access in a way that negatively impacts the organization. In an admissions context, this could involve altering transcripts, changing admission statuses, or leaking applicant data.

Threat modeling for insider risks typically categorizes them into several types:

1.  **Malicious Insiders:** Employees who intentionally misuse their access for personal gain, financial reward (e.g., bribery), or grievances.
2.  **Compromised Insiders:** Employees whose accounts or credentials have been taken over by an external attacker (e.g., through phishing or malware).
3.  **Negligent Insiders:** Employees who inadvertently cause a security breach through carelessness, such as misconfiguring a system or falling for a social engineering attack.

### Defensive Architecture and Controls

To mitigate the risks associated with compromised or malicious personnel, systems architects implement administrative and technical controls designed to limit the impact of any single individual.

#### 1. Separation of Duties (SoD)
SoD ensures that no single individual has complete control over a critical process. In an admissions process, this means the person who receives and verifies transcripts should not be the same person who makes the final admission decision or inputs that decision into the database. Requiring multiple approvals for critical actions reduces the risk of unilateral manipulation.

#### 2. Principle of Least Privilege (PoLP)
Users should only be granted the minimum level of access necessary to perform their job functions. An admissions officer reviewing applications for a specific department should not have access to financial aid records or the ability to modify the underlying database schema.

#### 3. Auditing, Logging, and Monitoring
Comprehensive auditing is critical for detecting and investigating insider threats.
*   **Immutable Logs:** Systems should generate logs that cannot be altered or deleted by the users generating the actions.
*   **Action Tracking:** Every significant action—such as viewing an applicant's file, changing a status, or exporting data—must be logged with the user's ID, a timestamp, and the specific data accessed.
*   **Anomaly Detection:** Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) systems can monitor these logs for unusual patterns. For example, an admissions official suddenly downloading a large number of records or accessing the system at unusual hours could trigger an alert.

#### 4. Data Loss Prevention (DLP)
DLP tools monitor and control the movement of sensitive data. They can prevent unauthorized exfiltration of applicant data, such as blocking the transfer of database records to external USB drives or unauthorized cloud storage services.

#### 5. Security Awareness and Reporting
Organizations implement training programs to educate staff on recognizing social engineering attempts, phishing, and the legal and ethical implications of bribery or data manipulation. Furthermore, establishing anonymous reporting mechanisms allows employees to report suspicious behavior or coercion attempts safely.
