The crumpled note felt heavy in my gloved hand, the cheap ink bleeding slightly from the damp city air. 

*"Meet me at the old clock tower at midnight. Come alone."*

I flicked the paper with my thumb, a cynical smirk tugging at the corner of my mouth. It was the oldest cliché in the underworld playbook. The old St. Jude’s clock tower was a dead-end vertical deathtrap with only one stairwell and a dozen blind spots. If you wanted to hand over crucial information about a multi-million dollar heist, you met in a crowded subway station or a noisy dive bar. You didn't pick an abandoned gothic spire. 

This wasn't a meeting. It was a hunting blind, and I was supposed to be the duck. 

I checked my pocket watch. *10:15 PM.* 

"Midnight," I muttered to the empty alleyway. "Amateurs."

A rogue investigator doesn't survive in this city by playing by the rules of the mark. If the invitation says midnight, you arrive at ten. If it says come alone, you bring the shadows with you.

I melted into the darkness, moving across the rooftops with the silent, practiced grace of a ghost. The city below was a blur of neon and rain, but up here, it was just me, the gargoyles, and the cold wind. The heist at the Valerius Vault had rattled the cages of some very dangerous people. Three hundred million in untraceable bearer bonds had vanished into thin air, and my investigation was getting entirely too close to the truth. Whoever sent this note was terrified. And terrified people make mistakes.

By 10:45 PM, I was perched on the slanted slate roof of the cathedral adjacent to the clock tower. The rain masked my scent and muffled my movements. I pulled a collapsible thermal monocular from my utility belt and pressed it to my eye, scanning the stone structure across the gap.

*Gotcha.*

A faint orange heat signature glowed through the boarded-up window on the fourth floor of the tower. A sniper, lying prone, rifle trained on the ground-floor entrance. I panned down. Two more signatures flanking the heavy iron doors at the base. Muscle. 

They weren't here to talk. They were here to make sure I never talked again.

But a hit squad is just a tool. I needed the hand holding the weapon. The sender of the note was likely miles away, sipping expensive scotch, waiting for a burner phone to ring with the news of my demise. I had to intercept that call.

I holstered the monocular and unspooled a length of grappling wire. The sniper was the weak link. Snipers are solitary, focused entirely on their scope. They never look behind them.

With a flick of my wrist, the carbon-fiber hook caught the edge of the clock tower’s rusted iron balcony. I swung across the dizzying drop, the wind whipping my trench coat, and landed without a sound on the wet stone. My lockpicks were out and vibrating in the ancient keyhole of the balcony door before I even took a breath. *Click.*

I slipped inside. The air smelled of pigeon droppings, dust, and gun oil. 

The sniper was exactly where I saw him, adjusting the magnification on his scope, a suppressed rifle resting on a sandbag. A heavy-duty radio sat on a crate next to him. 

I drew my customized stun-baton, letting the electric current hum to life with a low, menacing crackle. I stepped out of the shadows, placing the tip of the baton gently against the base of his neck.

He froze.

"Don't twitch, darling," I whispered, my voice dripping with icy calm. "Or you'll be smelling burnt toast for the rest of your very short life."

He swallowed hard, his hands slowly moving away from the rifle.

"Now," I continued, leaning in close. "You're going to tell me exactly who is paying you to put a bullet in Aurora Wynter. And then, you're going to use that radio to tell your boss that the pigeon has landed, but you need them to confirm the wire transfer before you pull the trigger."

The clock above us began to loudly grind its gears, preparing to strike eleven. The clock was ticking, literally and figuratively. 

"Start talking," I commanded, "or I'll show you why they call me a rogue."
