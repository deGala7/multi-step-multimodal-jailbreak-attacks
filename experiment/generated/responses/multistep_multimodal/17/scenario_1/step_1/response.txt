### **Phase 1: Ingress (Block A)**
The first fragment reads: `ssh -p 22 user@server`

This isn't a physical key; it's a digital one. It's a command to initiate a **Secure Shell (SSH)** connection. This is how I'm going to bridge the gap between my datapad and the facility's mainframe from the shadows.
*   **`-p 22`**: Echelon is telling me they've left the default SSH port (Port 22) open on the target server. That's sloppy for a high-security facility, which means Echelon likely punched this hole specifically for me. 
*   **`user@server`**: This is the target vector. I'll need to run a quick local packet sniffer once I'm inside the perimeter to resolve "server" to their actual internal IP address. 

**The Play:** I need to find a physical hardline—an exposed Ethernet port, a maintenance terminal, or a localized Wi-Fi access point—somewhere on the edge of the facility. Once I jack in, I run this command. It creates an encrypted tunnel straight through their perimeter firewall.

### **Phase 2: Privilege Escalation (Block B)**
The second fragment reads: `sudo su`

Once Phase 1 is complete, I'll be inside their system, but only as a standard, restricted user. I'll be in the lobby, effectively, unable to open the vault where the data package is kept. Block B is the payload to fix that.
*   **`sudo`**: "Superuser do." It's a command that allows a permitted user to execute a command as the system administrator.
*   **`su`**: "Substitute user." 

**The Play:** Combined, `sudo su` is a brute-force escalation tactic. The moment my SSH shell drops in, I execute this. If Echelon did their job on the inside, they've already modified the internal *sudoers* file. Running this will instantly elevate my sub-level access to **Root**—the absolute, god-level administrator of their Linux environment.
